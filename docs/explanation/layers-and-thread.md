# About layers and the thread

Long texts fail in two ways. Sentences go wrong: a vague word, a repetition, a clause that reads two ways. And the line goes wrong: a paragraph that belongs elsewhere, a section that argues something the book does not need, a step that is missing. Most editors help with the first kind and leave the second to the author's memory.

Throughline treats the text as four layers, and every part of the screen belongs to one of them.

| Layer | Question | Where it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Document | Do the sections follow one line from start to end? | Main line in the map, whole-text verdict, notes |
| Section | Does each paragraph serve the section and follow from the one before? | Outline, gists, the thread, the Line tab |
| Paragraph | Is the paragraph clear and does it say one thing? | The lens |
| Sentence | Does this sentence earn its place? | Sentence focus, sentence lengths in the lens |

The breadcrumb at the top names your position on every layer and switches between them. Switching changes what is dimmed. It does not change the text, so you can move up a layer to check the line and back down to write without losing your place.

## Why a thread

German has an idiom for a text's line of thought: *der rote Faden*, the red thread. The editor draws it literally. It runs through the outline and down the margin of the section you are in, one segment per paragraph.

The thread carries the two section-level judgements the model makes about each paragraph:

- **Bridge**: does this paragraph follow from the one before? A weak bridge makes the thread dashed at that paragraph.
- **Fit**: does this paragraph serve the section? A poor fit moves the paragraph's bead off the thread.

Each paragraph's bead also shows whether the model has read it: filled when read, a ring with a dot when you edited it since, a dashed ring when it has not been read yet. A small orange badge to the left counts the suggested edits for that paragraph; click it to open them in the lens. The Editor tab in the settings has a legend.

Both use the same visual, so you read the section's coherence at a glance without opening anything. The numbers behind them are in the lens and in the Line tab when you want them.

## Why the model does not write

The model points at problems and leaves the writing to you. Its suggestions are quotes from your text with a short reason, and a replacement only where one word or phrase is enough. That choice keeps your voice in the text. It also suits small local models, which judge passages more reliably than they write them.

## Why nothing needs a prompt

Every reading happens in the background, ordered by where you are. You never have to ask. The chat on the desk is there for questions the readings do not answer, such as whether an argument holds or which sources might help.
